Climate change: We need to be prepared for any eventuality

IN the Johor election, the Malaysian United Democratic Alliance raised climate change in its manifesto.

Party president Syed Saddiq Syed Abdul Rahman spoke at length on Twitter about the impact of severe climate change on the nation.

About the same time, Kuala Lumpur was inundated by floodwaters, and several east coast states experienced a second wave of flash floods.

Klang Valley folks were still reeling from flash floods that destroyed their livelihoods and properties a few months earlier. On March 10, landslides killed four people in Taman Bukit Permai in Ampang.

Syed Saddiq is right. We need to pay more attention to climate change. According to experts, soaring temperatures are likely to increase the occurrence of heatwaves.

Variations in precipitation may cause droughts and floods across the country. The rise in sea levels may inundate more coastal areas in Kelantan, Terengganu, Pahang and Johor.

All these are expected to have environmental and socio-economic effects, exacerbating environmental issues and reinforcing inequality. They will have a greater impact on humans, crops and food security.

Climate change needs to be addressed urgently. The problem is, we never learn from mistakes. Mother Nature has been lashing out at us from time to time but we still don't get it.

While natural disasters are inevitable, as history shows, that doesn't reduce our collective shock when they occur.

Surfing the Internet, I found news reports on natural disasters that made headlines last year.

HAITI was rocked by a 7.2-magnitude earthquake that killed more than 2,000 people and damaged tens of thousands of homes;

SUPER Typhoon Rai, with winds up to 193kph, killed 375 people in the Philippines;

LANDSLIDES in China's Henan province killed more than 300 people;

HISTORIC flooding resulted in more than 200 fatalities in Germany and Belgium; and,

HURRICANE Ida battered the Gulf Coast, killing 91 people across nine states in the United States.

Recently, several cities and smaller towns in New South Wales, Australia, were submerged in floodwaters from weeks of rainfall. And lives and properties were lost when the Eunice and Franklin storms lashed the United Kingdom last month.

EM-DAT, the international disaster database that aims to rationalise decision-making for disaster preparedness, states that in the past decade, approximately 60,000 people per year died from natural disasters.

This represented 0.1 per cent of total deaths worldwide. Across the globe, from 2010 to 2019, earthquakes killed 267,480 people, and extreme temperatures took 74,244 lives.

Floods caused 50,673 deaths; storms, 27,632; droughts, 20,120; landslides, 10,109; volcanic activity, 1,363; wildfires, 881; and mass movement, 100.

Throughout history, droughts and floods were the most fatal natural disasters. However, deaths from these events are relatively low now compared with earthquakes, which are by far the most deadly natural disaster in modern times.

In the past decade, earthquakes had killed more people worldwide than any other natural calamity.

However, the use of technology in disaster preparedness has resulted in a decline in deaths from natural disasters over a period of 100 years.

EM-DAT stated that in the 1920s, the world averaged more than 500,000 deaths from natural disasters per year. These were caused by several outlier events. The Tokyo earthquake in 1923 killed more than 146,000 people. Drought and famine killed three million people in China between 1928 and 1930.

In the 1930s, the average number of deaths per year dropped to below 500,000, but a number of events still stand out.

In 1931, floods in China killed more than 3.7 million people, and in 1935, an earthquake killed up to 60,000 people in Pakistan.

Over time, the decadal average dropped to fewer than 100,000 deaths per year. If we consider the rate of population growth, then the decline over the past century has been even more dramatic. For this reason, we need to relook at the climate change impact and be prepared for any eventuality.
